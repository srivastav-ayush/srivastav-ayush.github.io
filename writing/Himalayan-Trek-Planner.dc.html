The Himalayan Trek Calendar: Which Trek, Which Month, Which Year

Sixty-five treks, seven scoring dimensions, four quarters — and a set of sliders that lets you decide what actually matters to you, because the right trek for someone chasing views is not the right trek for someone chasing temples.

By Ayush·8 min read·April 2026

Every ranked list of Himalayan treks is really a ranking of whoever wrote it. I care disproportionately about views, and about whether there is something on the trail worth stopping for — a temple, a monastery, a village that has been there longer than the trail has. Someone training for a Himalayan peak cares about summit feeling and nothing else. Someone taking their parents cares about safety and food. Those three people should not be handed the same list. So this one is not fixed: seven sliders set how much each dimension counts, and every score, rank, and table on the page recomputes as you move them.

On the default weighting — mine — 12 treks clear 8.0 across the April 2026 → March 2027 trekking year, spread over all four quarters: one spring temple trek (Q1), one monsoon crossover (Q2), one Nepal or Sikkim autumn trek (Q3), one winter snow summit (Q4). For a first-timer it comes out as Q1 Chopta (4,000m debut) → Q4 Kedarkantha (3,800m winter summit) → Q2 Kashmir Great Lakes (4,000m monsoon) → Q3 Nepal, in that order. That is a starting point, not the answer — the sliders are two screens down.

Weight it yourself

Each slider sets how much you care about one dimension, from 0 (irrelevant) to 10 (this is the whole reason I am going). The percentages beside them are the normalised share of the total, so they add to exactly 100 no matter where you drag — you never have to take weight away from one thing to give it to another, and you can never end up at 103%.

Where the sliders disagree with the editorial score

For most treks the two numbers land within a few tenths of each other. Everest Base Camp is 9.1 editorially and about 8.8 on the default weights; Chopta is 8.7 and about 8.6. Then there is Chadar.

Chadar scores 9.0 editorially and roughly 7.0 on the seven dimensions. Nothing is broken — the dimensions are measuring what they say they measure. Chadar has middling views (a gorge, not a panorama), poor safety for a first-timer, and food that is whatever survives being carried in at −30°C. What it has instead is a thing no scoring rubric contains: it is a walk on a frozen river through a Himalayan gorge, and there is no substitute for it anywhere. The editorial 9.0 is that premium written down.

The honest reading is that the seven dimensions capture how good a trek is, and the editorial score captures how good a trek is plus how badly you would regret never having done it. When you move the sliders you are deliberately discarding the second part, which is exactly right if you are choosing between four treks that are all worth doing and you need a tie-break on your own terms. It is exactly wrong if you are choosing the one trek of your life.

So what? Use the sliders to shorten the list. Use the editorial column to break the final tie. If a trek ranks low on your weights but carries a large editorial premium, that is a signal to read about it rather than to discard it.

Start with the trek that matches who you are today, not the one you'll be in three years.

Set the sliders to what you actually care about, cap the altitude at 4,000m and the days at whatever leave you have, and take the trek at the top. Then come back next year with the ceiling raised.
