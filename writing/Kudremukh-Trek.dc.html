Kudremukh in a Day: 22 Km, and a Man Coming Back for His 31st Year Running

A dawn-to-dusk climb up Karnataka's horse-faced peak with Tripbae — shola forest, stream crossings, a summit storm, and a fellow trekker who's returned every single year for 31 years, summit or not.

Why Kudremukh

Kudremukh — "horse face" in Kannada, for the shape one of its cliffs traces from certain angles — is the centrepiece of Kudremukh National Park in Chikkamagaluru district, one of the Western Ghats' richest biodiversity pockets. The trail climbs out of the village of Kalasa, through dense shola forest, across a handful of stream crossings, and out onto open rolling grassland before the final steep pull to the summit ridge. It's usually rated moderate-to-difficult — less about technical climbing and more about sheer distance and elevation gain packed into one day, which is exactly what made ours a proper workout rather than a stroll. Numbers are capped by the forest department, so getting in requires a permit — which, it turns out, is the real challenge here.

The hardest part is the permit, not the trail. Kudremukh's forest department permits go live on the Arya Vighar website exactly two weeks before the date you want, at midnight — and the small daily quota is gone by around 12:15 AM. The site buckles under the load right when everyone's hitting refresh at once; ours went through, but it was as much luck as timing.

The Kalasa → Kudremukh route — roughly 11 km one-way, climbing from ~950 m to the 1,892 m summit

The climb

We started at 8:30 AM under overcast skies — the kind of grey, damp morning that's normal for the Western Ghats outside of peak season. The route alternates between shaded shola forest and completely open ridgeline, with a handful of stream crossings in between; the water was running fast and cold, high enough that a couple of the crossings needed real care with footing.

Up through the brush toward the ridge, then across Tholai Stream

There's no bridge at most of these crossings — you just walk straight through the water. My shoes were soaked within the first crossing and never really dried the rest of the day: right about the time they'd start to feel wearable again, the next stream would show up. I kept them on the whole way rather than risk bare feet — leech territory — and got lucky: not a single bite all day — a personal win I'm choosing to credit entirely to skill.

The leeches weren't a surprise, at least. A previous trek in Wayanad had already taught me that lesson the hard way, so this time I came prepared — trekking pants with cuffs that cinch tight at the ankle, and my t-shirt tucked firmly into my pants the whole day. Fully sealed at every entry point. Slightly ridiculous look, zero regrets.

Past the treeline the forest gives way to open grassland, and the trail keeps climbing along the ridge with the summit teasing in and out of the clouds ahead. For a single-day outing it's genuinely tough — there's no getting around the fact that 22 km round trip, 11 km up and 11 back down, with real elevation gain each way takes it out of you — but the terrain keeps changing often enough that it never drags.

The final ridge to the summit, cloud closing in
On the trail

Somewhere on the way up we fell in with a man who told us this was his 31st year coming back to this exact trek — one trip up Kudremukh, every single year, for 31 years running. He was in his 50s now, and told us plainly that he wouldn't be summiting this time — but he still comes back every year regardless, climbing as far as his body will take him that day. No fanfare about it, just a quiet fact dropped into conversation. It reframed the whole day: this was never a one-off tick on a list for him, it's a standing appointment he keeps with the same mountain, year after year, summit or not.

The storm at the top

By the time we reached the summit, the mist had thickened into full cloud cover — that high up, we were standing inside the same clouds we'd been watching roll over the ridge all morning. Orographic cloud like this is a normal feature of Western Ghats summits, especially through the monsoon months: moist air gets pushed up the slope, cools, and condenses right at altitude, so a socked-in peak is more the rule than the exception here. Kudremukh's namesake "horse face" cliff is supposed to be visible from certain angles near the top, but between the mist and the cloud we couldn't make it out at all — just white in every direction.

Then it properly rained — not a drizzle but close to a storm, heavy enough that we ended up eating lunch in about 15 minutes flat, half-sheltered, before packing up and rushing back down rather than lingering at the top. It was a reminder that the summit is the halfway point, not the finish line — the 11 km back down still had to happen, storm or not.

Socked in at the summit — mist and cloud, no horse-face in sight

The way back

Going down 11 km after already doing 11 up, in the rain, works a completely different set of muscles than the ascent did. The wet mud made every step downhill something to think about rather than just do, so the pace dropped right down — slow and deliberate beats fast and careless on a slick descent. Nothing went wrong, but a trekking pole would have made a real difference here, just for the extra point of contact and the confidence of not slipping; it's on the list for next time.

One entirely self-inflicted problem made the descent worse than it needed to be: I'd forgotten to cut my toenails before the trek. On a long, sustained downhill stretch your bodyweight rams forward into your toes with every step, and long nails turn that into real pain by the end — nothing serious, just an avoidable kind of miserable. Cut your nails before you go; future you, four hours from the finish, will be grateful.

Back at the forest outpost in Kalasa, right around 4:30

By the time that outpost — the same one we'd set out from that morning — came back into view, I was running on fumes: the whole trek done on about four hours of sleep the night before, barely walking straight by the end. There's an unofficial cutoff to be back by 5 PM, so seeing it appear right around 4:30 was a genuine wave of relief — on time, and the toe pain about to end. I got home, slept for most of the next day, and only felt properly normal again after that.

If you're going: book through a permitted operator (we went with Tripbae) and set a midnight alarm for exactly two weeks before your date to grab the Arya Vighar permit. Carry proper rain gear even if the forecast looks fine, bring a trekking pole for the descent, cut your toenails before you go, and start early enough to be well down before dark.

Soaked shoes, a stolen view, a storm on the summit, and someone else's 31st year on the same trail — not exactly the postcard version of Kudremukh, but a better story for it. Would I go back and do it in exactly this weather again? Without hesitation.

Trip details: Kudremukh Peak, Kudremukh National Park, Chikkamagaluru district, Karnataka. Trekked with Tripbae.
